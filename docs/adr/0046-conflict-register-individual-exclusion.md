# ADR-0046 - An optional conflict register: a firm may record that one of its people must not be added to a project, checked when someone is enrolled and nowhere else; it is an administrative guardrail against an accidental grant, NOT part of the access boundary, which the assembly environment already provides

**Status:** **Accepted, and deliberately not load-bearing** (2026-07-29; scope corrected 2026-07-30). Built as T-245/T-246. Raised by the Ask agent-harness Phase 0 permission interrogation ([docs/ask/phase-0-discovery.md](../ask/phase-0-discovery.md) finding F1), whose severity was **overstated** — see the correction below — and its three forks settled by owner ruling (§7–§9).

**Correction, owner-ruled 2026-07-30 — what this ADR does NOT do.** The original text argued that ADR-0026 closed the "machine path" for a consultant serving rival clients while "the human path is open". That was wrong, and it is the reason this control was briefly treated as a security finding. **The data leak it was reaching for cannot occur.** A request runs in exactly one assembly environment (ADR-0025) and the gateway carries no scope-widening parameter (ADR-0029, asserted by a compile-time test), so a person working for two clients cannot reach one client's material from inside the other's project, whatever they personally know. ADR-0026's *first* mechanism — "the environment makes another client's material unreachable elsewhere" — already covers this in full. What remains is a person's memory, which no access model governs and professional obligation already addresses.

So this register is an **administrative guardrail**: a firm records that a named person must not be added to a project, and the platform refuses the enrolment. That is its whole function. **It is not an input to access resolution** — §3 below is struck accordingly, and the re-check that briefly existed in `resolveAccess` has been removed. A request resolves on whether the user holds a live Participant on the project, and nothing else. The consequence is deliberate: if someone conflicted *is* on a project, they have access, and the fix is a human removing them — an administrative record must never silently withdraw access from someone mid-work.

Builds on ADR-0015 (the two-level ceiling), ADR-0016 (default-deny per deliverable), ADR-0018 (isolation as the standing default) and ADR-0033 (Membership and Participant as auditable intervals). Sits on the same axis as ADR-0026 (the C-serving-rival-clients threat) but at the human rather than the store level.

**Context:** *(as originally written, and left standing because the reasoning about the mechanism is still correct — only its importance was misjudged; read it with the correction above.)* Infora's ethical wall is a *negative* construct: isolation is the default and the owner opens visibility deliberately (ADR-0018). Every wall mechanism in the platform is a form of "you do not see this unless something grants it" — Membership as ceiling, Participant as grant, default-deny per deliverable, the acknowledgement gate, per-participant narrowing. That posture is strong, and the Phase 0 interrogation confirmed it is enforced where it claims to be: as SQL pre-filters, before retrieval, never in a prompt.

What it cannot express is an *exclusion*: **holding X positively bars Y.** ADR-0018 considered the adjacent case and concluded that "competing organisations on one project need no special wall, because they do not see each other's work unless the owner shares it." That reasoning is correct and does not reach the case here. The leak is not organisation-to-organisation on one project. It is **one person across two projects** — a consultant granted a Participant on a promoter's scheme and, later, on a scheme where their firm acts for an objector. Nothing in the platform prevents that grant, nothing flags it, and nothing surfaces it to anyone who would care. The wall never fires because there is nothing for it to fire against: both grants are individually legitimate.

This is the same threat axis ADR-0026 addresses — a consultancy legitimately serving rival interests — and ADR-0026 closed the *machine* path, ruling what may enter a firm's portable org context and prohibiting cross-organisation Signal pooling. ~~The *human* path is open.~~ **Struck 2026-07-30: that sentence was the error.** ADR-0026's other mechanism — the assembly environment — already closes the human path too, because a person cannot reach material outside the one environment their request runs in. What is genuinely absent is only the *record*: acting for a promoter on one scheme and an objector on another is routine consultancy practice, managed today by internal walls Infora has no way to represent or evidence. Representing them is useful. It is not a security control.

A consultancy's compliance lead may well ask about it, and being able to show the record is worth something in that conversation. But the honest answer to "can your platform stop our people leaking between clients" is the environment, not this register.

**Decision:**

**1. Exclusion is declared, never inferred.** Infora does not attempt to detect that two schemes are in conflict — it has no basis to, and guessing would be worse than not trying. A conflict is a **declared relation** between two projects (or two named sides of one project), asserted by a party with standing to assert it, and recorded as an audited row. Everything else in this ADR follows from a declaration existing.

**2. Checked at grant time, refusing with a reason.** Creating a Participant that would breach a live exclusion is refused at the point of creation, with a stated reason naming the exclusion — not the conflicting project, unless the requester is entitled to know it exists. A refusal is audited.

**3. ~~Re-checked in the predicate, as defence in depth.~~ Struck 2026-07-30 (owner-ruled).** The register is checked when someone is **enrolled**, and nowhere else. It is not an input to `resolveAccess`, and the re-check that briefly implemented this clause has been removed from the code. Two reasons. An administrative record must never silently withdraw access from someone already working — that is what §4 refuses for the retrospective case, and it would be incoherent to do by the back door at request time. And treating it as part of the access boundary misrepresents where the confidentiality guarantee comes from: the assembly environment (see the correction in Status), which holds whether or not any exclusion is ever declared.

**4. Retrospective declaration reports; it never silently revokes.** Declaring an exclusion where conflicting grants already exist **reports them** and does not revoke them. Revocation is a human act with a record. An automatic revocation could remove a specialist from an examination mid-round, and a wall control that causes an unattributed outage will be switched off rather than trusted.

**5. Currency by state, on the existing pattern.** An exclusion is an interval: declared, live, and withdrawn — never deleted. Past refusals stay attributable to the exclusion that caused them, exactly as a closed Participant interval keeps past attribution standing (ADR-0033).

**6. Audited end to end.** Who declared the exclusion and when; every grant it refused; every retrospective conflict it reported; who withdrew it and when. The audit of who-could-see-what reduces to the owner's sharing actions plus review appointments (ADR-0018) — this adds the exclusions to that same reckoning.

**7. Grain: individual by default, with an optional organisation-level form.** *(Fork A, owner-ruled 2026-07-29.)* The default exclusion binds a **named person**: this individual may not hold live Participant grants on both sides. This is what an internal wall actually is — a large consultancy routinely acts on both sides of two schemes with different teams, behind a wall it has maintained for years, and an organisation-grain-only control forbids a legitimate arrangement it has no standing to forbid. The consequence of forbidding it is not that the firm dissolves the arrangement; it is that one scheme leaves the platform and its confidentiality goes back to being managed on email, which is worse for everyone. A firm that genuinely does not want to act on both sides may additionally declare an **organisation-level exclusion**, which binds every Participant of that organisation. Where both forms are present the organisation-level one is the ceiling and the individual one narrows within it, on the same subset invariant as ADR-0015. The cost accepted: Infora holds a per-person conflict list, a heavier and more sensitive object than an organisation list, and it is protected accordingly — org-private on the ADR-0018 private floor, per the Consequences below.

**8. The consultancy's own org admin declares; the project owner may require an attestation, never declare.** *(Fork B, owner-ruled 2026-07-29.)* Only the firm holds both the information and the obligation. For a project owner to declare "these two schemes conflict for Org C", the owner must already know that Org C acts for the rival — and that is Org C's confidential engagement information, which in most cases Org C would be in breach for having disclosed. An owner-declared exclusion therefore requires the owner to know something it usually has no right to know, which is why the architectural analogy to ADR-0015/ADR-0030's composing ceilings does *not* transfer here. What the owner gets instead is an **attestation requirement**: a per-project setting obliging each member organisation to attest that it operates a conflict policy and has declared any exclusions its engagements require. The attestation is an audited record — who attested, for which organisation, when — and is verifiable *that* it exists without disclosing *what* it protects. This is deliberately a trust-plus-audit posture rather than a mechanical one, and it is the honest one: a client cannot inspect a supplier's internal wall, and a control that pretends otherwise would be theatre.

**9. A refusal names that a wall applies and nothing more.** *(Fork C, owner-ruled 2026-07-29.)* The refusal reads *"An ethical wall applies to this grant. Contact your organisation's administrator."* It confirms that a control fired, never which scheme or engagement it protects, and routes the person to the only party entitled to explain — their own org admin, who declared it and can see the register. The bare unexplained denial was rejected for a counter-intuitive reason worth recording: **it leaks more.** An unexplained refusal gets investigated — the requester rings the person, who also does not know, and it escalates to the project owner, who asks the consultancy why. The confidential fact surfaces through conversations within a day, in more inboxes than a scoped message would have reached, and with no audit trail. Naming the control without naming its subject is both the kinder answer and the containing one. **Symmetry:** an exclusion is symmetric by construction — the relation is unordered, so declaring X-excludes-Y and Y-excludes-X are the same row, and there is no asymmetric form to get wrong.

**Consequences:** A firm gains a way to record its own internal walls and have the platform respect them at enrolment. The access layer gains two tables — the exclusion register and the attestation record — and two check points, neither on a hot path. Infora becomes able to *represent and evidence* an internal wall that its customers already maintain by hand, which is a defensible answer to the compliance question rather than an assurance.

The per-person conflict list created by §7 is itself sensitive: it reveals which of a firm's people are walled from what, which is close to revealing the engagements. It is therefore org-private on the ADR-0018 private floor — readable by the declaring organisation's own admins only, never by a project owner and never across the wall — and the attestation (§8) exists precisely so an owner can gain assurance without gaining that list.

**What this does not buy, stated plainly so nobody re-argues it:** no confidentiality guarantee. That comes from ADR-0025's one-environment-per-request rule, which holds whether or not a single exclusion is ever declared. This register prevents an administrative accident and produces an audit record a compliance conversation can point at. Those are worth having; they are not a wall.

Two honest residuals, both accepted. First, **an exclusion is only as good as its declaration**: a conflict nobody declares is a conflict the platform cannot see. This reduces the problem from "structurally impossible to express" to "requires a deliberate, auditable act", the same posture ADR-0026 accepted for curation and ADR-0018 for owner sharing. Second, **the attestation is trust plus audit, not enforcement** — a firm can attest and be wrong. Both residuals are visible to a client rather than hidden by a control that overpromises, which is the point.

Deliberately not addressed: detecting undeclared conflicts; time-limited or decaying exclusions; and any cross-organisation view of one firm's register.
